###### Abstract

After extensive experimentation on the Translation, Confinement, and Sustainment rotating magnetic-field (RMF)-driven field reversed configuration (FRC) device [A. L. Hoffman _et al._, Fusion Sci. Technol. **41**, 92 (2002)], the principal physics of RMF formation and sustainment of standard prolate FRCs inside a flux conserver is reasonably well understood. If the RMF magnitude \(\bar{B}_{\omega}\) at a given frequency \(\omega\) is high enough compared to other experimental parameters, it will drive the outer electrons of a plasma column into near synchronous rotation, allowing the RMF to penetrate into the plasma. If the resultant azimuthal current is strong enough to reverse an initial axial bias field \(B_{o}\) a FRC will be formed. A balance between the RMF applied torque and electron-ion friction will determine the peak plasma density \(n_{m}\)\(\propto\)\(B_{\omega}\)/ \(\eta^{12}\omega^{12}\)\(r_{s}\), where \(r_{s}\) is the FRC separatrix radius and \(\eta\) is an effective weighted plasma resistivity. The plasma total temperature \(T_{i}\) is free to be any value allowed by power balance as long as the ratio of FRC diamagnetic current, \(\mid\)\(t\)\({}_{\rm{RMF}}^{\prime}\)\(-\)2\(B_{\omega}\)/\(\mu_{o}\), is less than the maximum possible synchronous current, \(\mid\)\(t\)\({}_{\rm{sync}}\)\(=\)\((n_{o})\)\(\omega\)\({}^{2}\)/2. The RMF will self-consistently penetrate a distance \(\delta\)\({}^{2}\) governed by the ratio \(\zeta\)\(=\)\(t\)\({}_{\rm{RMF}}^{\prime}\)\(\mid\)\(t\)\({}_{\rm{RMF}}^{\prime}\). Since the FRC is a diamagnetic entity, its peak pressure \(p_{\rm{=}}\)\(=\)\(n_{e}\)\(kT_{i}\) determines its external magnetic field \(B_{e}\)\(=\)\((2\,\mu_{o}\)\(p_{m})\)\({}^{1/2}\). Higher FRC currents, magnetic fields, and poloidal fluxes can thus be obtained, with the same RMF parameters, simply by raising the plasma temperature. Higher temperatures have also been noted to reduce the effective plasma resistivity, so that these higher currents can be supported with surprisingly little increase in absorbed RMF power, _C 2006 American Institute of Physics_. [DOI: 10.1063/1.2162052]

## I Introduction

Rotating magnetic fields (RMFs) have been employed to drive currents in plasmas for many decades,[1, 2] and their use has been extensively developed in a series of "rotamak" experiments at Flinders University in Australia.[3, 4] In this paper we will review the development of RMF current drive theory as it applies to prolate field-reversed configurations (FRCs) confined inside a flux conserving coil and develop an analysis based on the nonuniform resistivity profiles which appear to be a dominant characteristic of RMF-driven FRCs. In particular, this will allow us to estimate density and temperature dependencies of the plasma resistivity from experimental data. The effective plasma resistivity is the key parameter for determining RMF current drive effectiveness since it determines the plasma density that can be supported and the amount of RMF power needed to support a given FRC poloidal current.

## II Experimental arrangement and basic data

A sketch of the TCS experiment is shown in Fig. 2. The plasma chamber consisted of two 80 cm inner diameter (ID), 125-m long quartz tubes, connected at the midplane by a metal foil lined plastic section with ports allowing for vacuum access. A two-pass CO interferometer viewed the plasma across a diameter in this section and its double-pass measurement is called \(fnd\ell\). A single bolometer also viewed the plasma across a diameter in this section, and a thin 5 mm diameter probe could be inserted from the top with 31 small single-axis loops spaced every 2 cm from the end of the probe for the first 20 cm (usually inserted so the end loop was at \(r\)=0) and then every 1 cm for the final 20 cm. The probe could be oriented to measure the FRC poloidal field \(B_{z}(r)\), the horizontal field \(B_{z}(r)\) [assumed to represent \(B_{\phi}(r)\)], or a combination of the two. When the probe was oriented at a usual 45\({}^{\circ}\) to the axis, the steady field was attributed to \(B_{z}\), and the envelope of the component at the RMF frequency was attributed to the RMF \(B_{\phi}\). Based on pure \(B_{z}\) or \(B_{x}\) measurements, this methodology was accurate. Experiments were initiated with a puff of preionized deuterium yielding full pressures of order of 0.15-0.75 mTorr (\(n_{\frac{p}{2}}\)=1-5 \(\times\)10\({}^{19}\) m\({}^{-2}\)).

Various spectral lines were observed with monochrometers through the quartz tube, and calibrated measurements of D\({}_{\phi}\) (including H\({}_{\phi}\)) light were made through the quartz using narrow line filters. An axial array of flux and magnetic-field loops were included to measure the external field \(B_{z}\)(a) and the excluded flux \(\Delta\phi\)(b). The excluded flux radius is defined as \(r_{\Delta\phi}\)=(\(\Delta\phi/\pi B_{\phi}\))\({}^{1/2}\) (approximately equal to \(r_{s}\)), and the FRC length \(\ell_{\rm Pl}\) is defined as the plasma volume \(\sqrt{\Delta\phi}\)\(\sqrt{\pi\Delta\phi^{2}}\)\

of a FRC. This tuned frequency was different from that obtained in vacuum, and vacuum RMF magnitudes at the plasma tuned frequency were thus considerably lower than obtained with plasma. The value of RMF \(B_{w}\) used in analysis, and shown in data traces, is simply scaled from the antenna current as \(B_{w}\)(mT)=0.647(A).

Experiments with the original RMF antennas were run at four different frequencies between 83 and 258 kHz. Since the RF pulser voltage was limited to 14 kV (and usually run at 12 kV), and \(I_{\mathrm{ant}}\)=\(V_{\mathrm{ant}}\)/\(\omega L_{\mathrm{ant}}\)(\(L_{\mathrm{ant}}\)=1.8 \(\mu\)J), the maximum obtainable values of \(B_{w}\) decreased with frequency. Since the plasma density scales approximately as \(B_{w}\)/\(\omega^{1/2}\), the obtainable plasma densities also decreased strongly with \(\omega\). However, since radiation losses were lower at the lower densities, higher temperatures could be achieved at higher \(\omega\). The axial bias field was provided by (20) 56-turn coils energized in parallel to act as a flux converter. Their effective flux conserving radius was 47 cm. The experiments shown were conducted with additional copper straps placed directly on the plasma tube wall at a radius of about 41.5 cm to serve as a "secondary" short-time-scale flux converter. Both the rise time of the multiturn coils and the \(L/R\) flux bleed through time (in vacuum) of the copper straps were about 4 ms. In 83 was measured by pickup loops located under the copper straps, while \(B_{z}\) shown in data traces was taken from the last internal probe loop at \(r\)=40 cm. The firing time of the RMF with respect to the bias coil initiation was set to try and make the fields inside and outside the copper strap flux rings equal during the FRC lifetime. This resulted in an effective flux conserving radius of \(r_{c}\)=\(r_{s}/(1-B_{o}/B_{w})^{1/2}\)\(\approx\) 45 cm. TCS could be run with an initial FRC translated from a small attached theta pinch, but all experiments described here utilized simple RMF formation from a preionized plasma.

Typical data at a midrange RMF frequency of 152 kHz (\(\omega\)=9.55\(\times\)10\({}^{5}\) rad/s) are shown in Fig. 3. The RMF was only applied for 1.5 ms for these discharges, which was long enough to reach quasisteady conditions, and provided the most accurate interferometer data. Other than due to the decay in RMF magnitude due to its finite capacitor bank energy source, the FRC would persist essentially unchanged for discharges lasting up to 10 ms.[5] Shots 9218 and 9217 were taken with an initial bias field of about 3 mT, rising to about 4 mT at 1 ms after RMF initiation. The \(B_{e}\) trace, taken at the center of TCS, was initially negative due to the induced currents in the copper flux rings, but was about the same as the \(B_{z}\)(\(r\)=40 cm) trace once quasisteady conditions were obtained. The initial \(B_{z}\) level indicates the true initial bias field. The RMF pulser voltage was 9 kV for shot 9218, and 12 kV for all other discharges (except 9751 where it was 6 kV) shown in this paper. The RMF magnitude increased approximately linearly with pulser voltage except when the plasma absorption was very high and the pulser tube losses became significant. Shot 9214 was run with a lower initial gas puff fill pressure, and also at a slightly higher bias field, as was the vacuum reference shot (bias field supply voltage increased from 180 to 220 V). The FRC excluded flux radius decreases with increasing bias field, and was purposely kept large for most experiments to avoid \(n\)=2 plasma distortions. ## VI Summary and Conclusions

RMF current drive with partial penetration occurs naturally due to values of \(\zeta\)=\(I^{\prime}{}_{\rm dis}/I^{\prime}{}_{\rm sync}\)\(\propto\)\(B_{e}/n_{e}\omega{}_{s}{}^{2}\) being less than unity. When using RMF to drive currents in FRCs, partial penetration is desirable since it both maximizes the applied torque on the electrons [at \(\delta^{\prime}/r_{s}\) values of about 0.5 (Ref. 7)] and minimizes the tendency of the radial component of the RMF to open field lines since the ratio of \(B_{e}/B_{\omega}\) is approximately proportional to \(\delta^{\prime}/r_{s}\). Low \(B_{r}\) also minimizes ion current drive in the RMF direction since it is actually \(B_{r}/em_{i}\) which should be small compared to \(\omega\). The inward radial forces produced by the partially penetrated RMF have also been seen to produce strong stabilizing forces against interchange instabilities, such as the FRC rotating \(n\)=2 instability.[20]

Low \(\zeta\) and partial penetration occur when the basic current drive parameter \(\omega{}_{s}{}^{2}\) is large relative to \(B_{e}/n_{e}\)\(\propto(T_{t}/n_{e})^{1/2}\) as it has been for past experiments where \(T_{t}\) was relatively low. Reducing \(\omega{}_{s}{}^{2}\) tends not to increase \(\zeta\) since


<!-- Page 17 -->

the driven FRC density increases and \(T_{t}\) tends to decrease. Restricting the total particle supply would not lower the density or \(\zeta\) for elongated FRCs since the FRCs would just shrink axially, unless the particle restriction contributed to higher temperatures. Based on the observed resistivity scaling, full penetration in standard flux-confined prolate FRCs will only occur when the ratio of temperature to density is significantly increased. If \(\zeta\) then becomes too close to unity, which would reduce the RMF drive torque, or even reverse it if \(\omega_{e}\) exceeded \(\omega\), the RMF frequency can be raised to maintain the partially penetrated RMF condition.

Under partial RMF penetration, many experimental measurements show that the RMF-driven FRC resistivity profile is highly peaked near the edge (where \(\int\eta j_{e}^{\ 2}\) will thus be large). These include the occurrence of edge-driven modes and the high ratio of absorbed RMF power to that which would be calculated based on \(\int\eta j_{e}^{\ 2}\) alone, with the resistivity calculated from torque balance. This may be due partially to the RMF drive process itself, but is also suggestive of a Chodura-type resistivity, with \(\eta_{\rm chod}\sim[1.05\)\(\times10^{-3}/n_{e}^{\ 1/2}(10^{19}\) m\({}^{-3})][1-\exp(-\omega_{e}r/3v_{s})]\)\(\Omega\) m and a relatively large \(\omega_{e}r/3v_{s}\) streaming parameter. In TCS \(3v_{s}\sim1.5\)\(\times10^{5}\) m/s for \(T_{t}\sim50\) eV and \(\omega_{e}r\) ranges from about 2\(-\)6 \(\times10^{5}\) m/s near the FRC edge, and is much lower in the interior. If the resistivity obeys the Chodura formula, as it has been observed to do for unsustained FRCs, this anomalous resistivity alone could account for the observed behavior. The improved performance seen as the total temperature and ion sound speed increase would tend to support the anomalous resistivity explanation. Chodura-type resistivity would also favor scaling to larger sizes where the current density would be lower for a given \(B_{e}\), and the streaming parameter would thus be lower. There is some evidence of this size scaling in extrapolating from the smaller STX experiment. Using a RMF with \(f_{\omega}\)=350 kHz, \(B_{\omega}\)=2 mT, and \(\delta^{\prime}/r_{s}\sim0.25\) to produce a \(r_{s}\)=20 cm FRC, the resultant peak density \(n_{m}\) was about \(0.5\times 10^{19}\) m\({}^{-3}\)[17] only about half of the Eq. (11) TCS value, implying that the effective resistivity dropped by about a factor of 4 in scaling from the 40 cm diameter STX to the 80 cm diameter TCS.

Experimental data so far do not indicate that the appearance or lack of appearance of the edge-driven mode has any effect on the overall RMF current drive performance. RMF current drive tends to drive the outer electrons into near synchronous rotation, with the remainder of the electrons being in approximate rigid-rotor rotation at a much lower frequency. Varying degrees of reduced azimuthal currents and shallower \(B_{z}(r)\) profiles near the field null have been seen, again without any noticeable effect on overall current drive performance as regards achieved densities and RMF power dissipation. The most significant results are that the achievable plasma density is well represented by Eq. (10), \(n_{e}\)\(\proptoB_{\omega}/\omega^{1/2}r_{s}\eta^{1/2}\), with the resistivity scaling as \((n_{e}T_{t})^{-1/2}\). The lowering of effective resistivity with temperature results in RMF being able to drive hotter, higher current and higher-magnetic-field FRCs without need to increase \(B_{\omega}\), and with only modest increases in absorbed RMF power.

The exciting results obtained with antisymmetric RMF current drive [16] can also be interpreted in the light of the scaling reported in this paper. The higher temperatures can be simply accounted for by reduction in thermal losses, but the observation of producing the same peak density, and absorbing the same RMF power, despite higher azimuthal currents (\(B_{e}^{\ 2}\) almost doubled) with what should be a less efficient current drive geometry, can only be accounted for by a plasma resistivity decreasing with temperature.

FRCs are an extremely attractive fusion confinement scheme due to their high beta and simple geometry. RMF provides a simple mechanism for forming and sustaining FRCs at the low densities required for steady-state operation. It also provides significant plasma heating and could be used alone to sustain FRCs, or in conjunction with other current drive and heating technologies, such as tangential neutral beam injection (TNBI). TNBI would be an ideal complement to RMF since it can drive current at the field null, and also counteract the torque the RMF exerts on the plasma. In order to successfully apply TNBI to a TCS-sized device the FRC poloidal flux level has to be raised from the previous 2 mWb to about 6 mWb in order to confine axis encircling charge-exchange ions within the FRC separatrix. [21] This will occur naturally if the temperature can be increased. Higher-temperature operation will be tested in a new device, TCS/ upgrade, which will employ antisymmetric RMF current drive and a greatly upgraded vacuum system to reduce impurity levels and radiation losses.
