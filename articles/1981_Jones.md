Steady-state solutions for the penetration of a rotating magnetic field into a plasma column

By IEUAN R. JONES AND WAHEED N. HUGRASS+

School of Physical Sciences, The Flenders University of South Australia,

South Australia, 5042

(Received 22 September 1980)

The penetration of an externally applied rotating magnetic field into a plasma cylinder is examined. Steady-state solutions of an appropriate set of magneto-fluid equations show that, provided the amplitude and rotation frequency of the field are suitably chosen, the penetration is not limited by the usual classical skin effect. The enhanced penetration of the rotating field is accompanied by the generation of a unidirectional azimuthal electron current which is totally absent in a purely resistive plasma cylinder.

## 1 Introduction

The magnetic field in some magnetic confinement schemes is, in part, generated by currents flowing in the plasma. In most cases this plasma current is produced by magnetic induction. Although induction coupling is efficient and relatively simple, it cannot be used to sustain steady-state operation. For this and other reasons there has recently been a renewed interest in studying alternative methods of driving plasma currents, especially methods based on radio-frequency (RF) techniques (see, for example, Fukuda & Matsuura 1978; Fisch 1978; Wong 1979; McWilliams _et al._ 1980).

The technique of generating azimuthal electron currents in a plasma column by means of rotating magnetic fields was first investigated by Blevin & Thonemann (1962). The principles of their technique can best be understood as follows.

Suppose that a transverse rotating magnetic field completely penetrates a cylindrical plasma column (figure 1). Provided that the angular frequency, \(\omega\), of the rotating field lies between the ion and electron cyclotron frequencies (\(\omega_{ci}\), \(\omega_{eq}\); calculated with reference to the amplitude of the rotating field, \(B_{u}\)) and provided the electron collision frequency is less than the electron cyclotron frequency, the electrons can be considered as 'tied' to the lines of force of the rotating field. They circulate synchronously with angular frequency \(\omega\), whereas the ions (at least in the short term) have no net azimuthal motion. The electrons thus form a steady azimuthal current (in fact, the Hall current). In most systems, Hall currents are inhibited by electric polarization fields. In the situation under discussion, however, charge separation does not occur because of the azimuthal symmetry.


<!-- Page 2 -->

In discussions about the Blevin-Thonemann mechanism, comments having the following general tenor frequently arise: 'While the above physical picture is particularly beguiling, everyone knows that the classical skin effect will prevent the rotating field from penetrating the plasma column in the first instance!' The purpose of this paper is to demonstrate the existence of steady-state solutions to an appropriate set of magnetofluid equations which show that the penetration of a rotating field into a plasma column is not limited by the usual classical skin effect.

For the particular choice of field amplitude and rotation frequency made in the above description of the Blevin-Thonemann mechanism, the appropriate form of Ohm's law is

\[{\bf E}=\eta{\bf j}+\frac{1}{ne}({\bf j}\times{\bf B}).\]

Steady-state solutions have been obtained in two limits. First, a situation is examined where the \(\eta{\bf j}\) term in Ohm's law dominates. The basic tendency is then for induced axial plasma currents to screen out the applied rotating field in a distance equal to the classical skin depth. It is shown, however, that the inclusion of only a small contribution from the \({\bf j}\times{\bf B}\) term leads to the generation of azimuthal currents in the plasma column and that the presence of these currents, in turn, aids the further penetration of the rotating field.

The second limit which has been examined considers the \({\bf j}\times{\bf B}\) term in Ohm's law to predominate. The solution, originally obtained by Blevin & Thonemann (1962), which describes the complete and almost complete penetration of the

## 2 The basic model and equations

Consider an infinitely long plasma cylinder of radius \(R\) lying in a uniform axial magnetic field, \(B_{a}\). A uniform transverse magnetic field which rotates about the cylindrical axis is applied to this plasma column. The amplitude, \(B_{a}\), and the angular frequency, \(\omega\), of this rotating field are chosen so that

\[\omega_{ei}\ll\omega\ll\omega_{ee}, \tag{1}\]

where \(\omega_{ei}\) and \(\omega_{ee}\) are the ion and electron cyclotron frequencies calculated with respect to the amplitude of the rotating field.

An \((r,\theta,z)\) system of co-ordinates is chosen and MKS units are used throughout.

The following assumptions are made:

1. All quantities are assumed independent of \(z\).
2. The ions form a uniformly distributed neutralizing background of fixed, massive positive charges.
3. The plasma resistivity, \(\eta\), is taken to be a scalar quantity which is constant in time and uniform in space. In particular, \(\eta\) is assumed to be of the form

\[\eta=mv_{ei}/ne^{2}, \tag{2}\]

where \(m\) is the electron mass, \(e\) is the electronic charge, \(n\) is the uniform electron number density and \(v_{ei}\) is the electron-ion momentum transfer collision frequency.
4. The electron inertia is neglected; that is, it is assumed that \(\omega\ll\omega_{ee}v_{ei}\).
5. Displacement current is neglected. This implies that only systems for which \(\omega R/c\ll 1\), where \(c\) is the speed of light, are considered.

Within the above constraints, the system is completely described by the self-consistent solutions to the following set of equations: Maxwell's equations,

\[\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=-\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t, \tag{3}\] \[\nabla\times\mathbf{B}=\mu_{0}\mathbf{j}; \tag{4}\]

the equation of motion for the electrons,

\[\frac{-e}{m}\left[\mathbf{E}+(\mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B})\right]-v_{ei} \mathbf{u}=0, \tag{5}\]

where \(\mathbf{u}\) is the velocity of the electron fluid; and the definition of current density,

\[\mathbf{j}=-ne\mathbf{u}. \tag{6}\]


<!-- Page 4 -->

The solutions for the field quantities must satisfy the appropriate boundary conditions. In particular, for \(r\geq R\) they must reduce to the equations describing the externally applied magnetic field,

\[B_{r} =B_{\omega}\cos{(\omega t-\theta)}, \tag{7}\] \[B_{\theta} =B_{\omega}\sin{(\omega t-\theta)},\] (8) \[B_{z} =B_{a}. \tag{9}\]

Equations (2), (5) and (6) can be combined to form the appropriate Ohm's law for the problem,

\[\mathbf{E}=\eta\mathbf{j}+\frac{1}{ne}(\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B}). \tag{10}\]

By introducing the magnetic vector potential, \(\mathbf{A}\), where

\[\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}, \tag{11}\]

and making use of the assumption that \(\partial/\partial z\equiv 0\), it is straightforward to show that all the field quantities can be derived from the \(z\) components of \(\mathbf{B}\) and \(\mathbf{A}\). These components, \(B_{z}\) and \(A_{z}\), themselves satisfy the following pair of coupled partial differential equations,

\[\frac{\partial A_{z}}{\partial t}=\frac{\eta}{\mu_{0}}\nabla^{\mathbf{z}}A_{z }+\frac{1}{ne\mu_{0}r}\left(\left(\frac{\partial A_{z}}{\partial r}\right) \left(\frac{\partial B_{z}}{\partial\theta}\right)-\left(\frac{\partial A_{z} }{\partial\theta}\right)\left(\frac{\partial B_{z}}{\partial r}\right)\right) \tag{12}\]

and

\[\frac{\partial B_{z}}{\partial t}=\frac{\eta}{\mu_{0}}\nabla^{\mathbf{z}}B_{z }+\frac{1}{ne\mu_{0}r}\left(\left(\frac{\partial A_{z}}{\partial\theta}\right) \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\nabla^{\mathbf{z}}A_{z}-\left(\frac{\partial A_{z }}{\partial r}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\nabla^{\mathbf{z}}A_{z} \right), \tag{13}\]

where

\[\nabla^{2}\equiv\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r\frac{\partial}{ \partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^{3}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}}.\]

The nonlinear terms which appear on the right-hand side of (12) and (13) arise from the Hall term in Ohm's law (10).

## 5 Discussion

This paper demonstrates the existence, for the plasma model described in SS2, of steady-state solutions for which an applied rotating magnetic field penetrates into a plasma column to an extent greater than that predicted by the usual


<!-- Page 12 -->

classical skin effect. The larger the value of \(\omega_{ce}/\nu_{et}\), and hence the more important the influence of the nonlinear Hall term in the appropriate Ohm's law, the greater is the penetration of the field. In the limit where \(\omega_{ce}/\nu_{et}\gg 1\), the penetration is complete.

The question of accessibility of these steady-state solutions can only be answered by a numerical treatment of the initial-value problem. Such a treatment, which considers arbitrary contributions from the \(\eta\mathbf{j}\) and \(\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B}\) terms, is presented in a companion paper (Hugrass & Grimm 1981).

The enhanced penetration of the rotating field is accompanied by the generation of an azimuthal electron current which is totally absent in a purely resistive plasma column. This current can be conveniently observed in practice by measuring the quantity \(\Delta B_{z}\). The maximum value of the azimuthal current, and hence \(\Delta B_{z}\), is attained when all the electrons in the plasma column rotate synchronously with the field. In figure 2 the full curve shows the numerically computed dependency of \(\Delta B_{z}/\Delta B_{z}^{M}\) on the ratio \(\omega_{ce}/\nu_{ei}\) for one given set of conditions. The dotted curves are plots of the analytic expressions (40) and (72). Good agreement is obtained in the ranges of validity. It is seen that, for small values of \(B_{z}\) (small values of \(\omega_{ce}\)), the rotating field only partially penetrates the plasma column, only a fraction of the electrons rotate and consequently the value of \(\Delta B_{z}/\Delta B_{z}^{M}\) is small. On increasing the value of \(B_{z}\), a threshold regime is reached where \(\Delta B_{z}/\Delta B_{z}^{M}\) increases rapidly. Above a certain value of \(B_{z}\) there is no gain to be obtained by further increasing the amplitude of the rotating field since a situation has already been reached where \(\Delta B_{z}/\Delta B_{z}^{M}\approx 1\).

The rotating-field technique can thus be used to generate unidirectional


<!-- Page 13 -->

plasma currents. However, in a system where an electron current is driven by a rotating field, the ions are set into rotation by electron-ion collisions and, in a completely isolated plasma, both ions and electrons must ultimately rotate synchronously with the field. The characteristic time constant for the ions to come into rotation is \(m_{i}/m_{e}v_{e\xi}\). It would thus appear that ion rotation poses a problem if one wishes to drive a plasma current for times greater than the ion rotation time constant. It may transpire, however, that in practice ion momentum relaxation mechanisms such as charge exchange will ensure a constant net current.

We wish, finally, to discuss the application of a rotating field to a metallic conductor. Recall that the resistivity of an electrical conductor is proportional to \(v_{ei}/n\). A plasma is a good conductor by virtue of having a low value for \(v_{ei}\); a metallic conductor, on the other hand, owes its good conduction properties to a very high density of conduction electrons. The large value for \(v_{ei}\) normally encountered in metallic conductors implies that the nonlinear mechanism discussed in this paper can only become operative in such conductors if the amplitude of the rotating field lies in the megagauss range.
